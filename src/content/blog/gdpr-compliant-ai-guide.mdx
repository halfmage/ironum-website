Every time a European company sends customer data, employee records, or proprietary documents to a US-hosted AI API, it creates a GDPR compliance risk. Most businesses know this intuitively but proceed anyway because the alternatives seemed immature or expensive. That calculation has changed. In 2026, sovereign AI deployment is a viable option for many use cases, and often the simpler one to defend.

## The Core Problem: Why US Cloud AI Creates GDPR Risk

GDPR restricts the transfer of personal data to countries outside the European Economic Area (EEA). Transfers are allowed to countries with an adequacy decision, or with safeguards such as Standard Contractual Clauses. For the United States, the EU-US Data Privacy Framework (DPF) of 2023 is an adequacy decision that covers only US companies certified under it. The EU General Court upheld the DPF on 3 September 2025 (Case T-553/23), and an appeal is pending at the Court of Justice (Case C-703/25 P). The DPF is valid today, but its long-term future is not settled.

Here is why this matters for AI specifically:

**AI APIs process data in ways that go beyond simple storage.** When you send a document to an AI API for summarization, classification, or chat, the content is processed by models running on the provider's infrastructure. Even if the provider claims not to retain data, the processing itself constitutes a data transfer under GDPR.

**Model training and improvement create additional risks.** Some AI services may use input data for model improvement, depending on the plan and settings. Check the terms for the exact service tier you use. This means your confidential business data or customer personal data could influence future model outputs accessible to other users.

**Sub-processor chains are opaque.** Major AI providers rely on complex infrastructure involving multiple sub-processors, sometimes across jurisdictions. Keeping track of where your data actually goes, which GDPR requires you to document, takes real effort.

**US surveillance laws persist.** FISA Section 702 and Executive Order 12333 give US intelligence agencies broad authority to access data held by US companies, including data stored in European data centers operated by US companies. The DPF introduced new safeguards and a redress mechanism. Critics have challenged the framework in court, and the Court of Justice invalidated its two predecessors (Safe Harbor in Schrems I, Privacy Shield in Schrems II).

## The Risk Is Not Theoretical

European data protection authorities look closely at AI. In 2023 the Italian Garante temporarily restricted ChatGPT in Italy over GDPR concerns.

Where a US provider is not certified under the DPF, or the DPF does not cover a transfer, you need another transfer mechanism, usually Standard Contractual Clauses, together with a transfer impact assessment and, where needed, supplementary measures. Even with the DPF in place, you remain responsible for a legal basis, a data processing agreement and documenting where the data goes.

## What "GDPR-Compliant AI" Actually Requires

True GDPR compliance for AI goes beyond choosing an EU data center region. Here is what it demands:

### Data Residency Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

Processing and storing data within the EEA (or a country with an adequacy decision) avoids the transfer rules altogether. But data residency alone does not remove every risk if the provider is a US company subject to US jurisdiction. The legal entity controlling the infrastructure matters as well as the physical location.

### A Valid Legal Basis for Processing

For most enterprise AI use cases, the legal basis will be either legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f)) or contract performance (Article 6(1)(b)). If you process special categories of data (health, biometric, political opinions), you need explicit consent or another Article 9 exception. Using AI to process employee data requires careful analysis under Article 88 and national employment law.

### Data Minimization in Practice

GDPR's data minimization principle means you should send the minimum data necessary to achieve your purpose. In practice, this means:

- Anonymizing or pseudonymizing data before sending it to AI systems where possible
- Implementing data preprocessing pipelines that strip unnecessary personal data
- Using local models for sensitive data processing and cloud models only for non-sensitive tasks
- Never sending raw database exports or complete customer records to AI APIs

### Transparency and Data Subject Rights

If you use AI to process personal data, data subjects have the right to know. Your privacy notice must explain what AI systems you use, what data they process, and for what purpose. If AI-based profiling or automated decision-making affects individuals, Articles 13-15 and 22 impose specific information and objection rights.

### Data Processing Agreements

Any AI provider processing personal data on your behalf must sign a Data Processing Agreement (DPA) under Article 28. This DPA must specify the nature and purpose of processing, the types of data involved, the categories of data subjects, the provider's security measures, and sub-processor arrangements. Check whether the provider's standard DPA covers all of these points for your use case.

## How to Keep EU Data in the EU in an AI Pipeline

"EU region" on one service is not enough. An AI pipeline has more steps than most teams expect, and personal data can leave the EU at any of them. Check each one:

| Step | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Source data and storage** | Database, file storage and backups in an EU data center |
| **Embedding** | The embedding model runs in the EU, not through a global API endpoint |
| **Vector index** | The vector database is hosted in the EU, including replicas |
| **Model inference** | The language model runs in the EU; "global" deployment types may route requests to other regions |
| **Logs and monitoring** | Prompt logs, traces and error reports stay in the EU, or contain no personal data |
| **Sub-processors** | Every sub-processor in the DPA, with its location and legal entity |
| **Training** | Model training on your inputs is switched off in the contract, not only in a setting |

The last row in every check is the legal entity. Data in a Frankfurt data center operated by a US company can still be subject to US access requests. If that risk matters for your data, consider providers that are EU legal entities, or run the pipeline on your own servers. ## Switzerland vs. EU: FADP and GDPR for AI Services

Swiss companies, and EU companies with Swiss customers or providers, ask how the Swiss rules compare. Switzerland's revised Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) has applied since 1 September 2023 ([Fedlex, SR 235.1](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/en)). The Swiss regulator, the FDPIC, states that the law is technology-neutral and applies directly to AI-supported processing ([FDPIC: AI and data protection](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/en/ai-and-data-protection)).

The two laws follow the same ideas. The differences that matter for an AI project:

| Topic | EU (GDPR) | Switzerland (FADP) |
|---|---|---|
| **Data breach notification** | To the authority within 72 hours (Art. 33) | To the FDPIC "as soon as possible" if there is a high risk (Art. 24) |
| **Sanctions** | Administrative fines on the company, up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (Art. 83) | Criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000 on the responsible individuals, for intentional violations (Art. 60 to 63) |
| **Impact assessment** | DPIA for likely high risk (Art. 35) | DPIA for likely high risk (Art. 22) |
| **Automated decisions** | Right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal or similar effects (Art. 22) | Duty to inform about automated individual decisions and right to have them reviewed by a person (Art. 21) |
| **Transfers to the US** | EU-US Data Privacy Framework for certified companies | Swiss-US Data Privacy Framework for certified companies, since 15 September 2024 ([FDPIC](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/en/15082024-new-swiss-us-data-privacy-framework)) |
| **Representative for foreign companies** | EU representative in some cases (Art. 27) | Swiss representative in some cases (Art. 14) |

Data flows between the EU and Switzerland are the easy part. The European Commission recognises Switzerland as adequate and confirmed this in its review of 15 January 2024 ([European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_24_161)). An EU company can use an AI provider in Switzerland without Standard Contractual Clauses, and Swiss data can flow to the EU.

A Swiss company is not automatically outside the GDPR. If it offers services to people in the EU or monitors their behaviour, the GDPR applies as well (Art. 3(2) GDPR). In practice, many Swiss companies with EU customers design their AI setup to meet both laws. Which rules apply to your company is a question for your data protection officer or legal advisers.

## The Sovereign AI Alternative

The good news is that 2026 is the year sovereign AI becomes genuinely practical for European businesses of all sizes. Several developments have converged:

### Open-Source Models Have Closed the Gap

Models like Llama 3.1, Mistral Large, and Qwen 2.5 deliver performance that rivals proprietary APIs for most enterprise use cases. For document processing, customer support, internal knowledge management, and workflow automation, open-source models running on your own infrastructure are no longer a compromise. ### European Infrastructure Is Ready

German and European cloud providers offer GPU-equipped infrastructure suitable for AI workloads. Hetzner, IONOS, OVHcloud, and Deutsche Telekom's Open Telekom Cloud all provide NVIDIA GPU instances in European data centers operated by European legal entities. With a European operator, there is no US parent company in the chain.

### Deployment Complexity Has Decreased

Tools like Ollama, vLLM, and text-generation-inference have made deploying and serving open-source models dramatically simpler. What required a specialized ML engineering team two years ago can now be accomplished by a competent DevOps engineer. Managed platforms (including Ironum) reduce the barrier even further.

### Total Cost of Ownership Favors Self-Hosting at Scale

For companies processing significant volumes of data through AI, self-hosted models are typically cheaper than API calls. A dedicated GPU server running Mistral or Llama costs a fixed monthly amount regardless of usage volume, while API costs scale linearly. At high volumes, the break-even point can come early. Calculate it with your own usage numbers.

## Practical [Deployment Options](/platform/deployment/)

European companies have several paths to GDPR-compliant AI:

**On-premises deployment:** AI models run on your own hardware in your own data center. The most control over data location and access. Best for companies with existing data center infrastructure and strict regulatory requirements (finance, healthcare, government contractors).

**Private cloud deployment:** AI models run on dedicated servers in a European cloud provider's data center. You get the control of self-hosting without managing physical hardware. Suitable for most enterprises that need compliance without the overhead of bare-metal management.

**Hybrid deployment:** Sensitive data processing happens on-premises or in a private cloud, while non-sensitive tasks use cloud APIs. This optimizes cost while protecting critical data. Requires careful architectural design to prevent data leakage between tiers.

## Making the Transition

If your company currently relies on US-based AI APIs, here is a practical migration path:

1. **Audit your current AI usage.** Document every AI service, what data it processes, and whether that data includes personal data or confidential business information.
2. **Classify data sensitivity.** Not all AI use cases involve personal data. Marketing copy generation, code assistance, and general research may pose lower risks than customer data processing or HR analytics.
3. **Prioritize high-risk use cases.** Move the most sensitive AI processing to sovereign infrastructure first. Customer-facing chatbots processing personal queries, document analysis involving personal data, and HR tools should be migrated first. For the chatbot piece specifically, our [GDPR-compliant chatbot playbook for SMEs](/resources/blog/chatbot-own-data-gdpr-sme/) walks through architecture, cost ranges, and a four-week pilot plan.
4. **Select sovereign infrastructure.** Choose a deployment model (on-premises, private cloud, or hybrid) and an infrastructure provider subject to EU jurisdiction.
5. **Test and validate.** Run your sovereign AI deployment in parallel with existing services to validate performance before switching over.
